01

One score hides the argument

When I prioritise, I score initiatives on five axes rather than one: user value, business impact, engineering effort, strategic fit, and growth potential. Collapsing those into a single number feels decisive and quietly destroys the useful information.

Two initiatives can land on the same composite score for opposite reasons. One is cheap and strategically irrelevant, the other is expensive and central. A single number makes them look interchangeable. Five columns make the disagreement visible, which is the only way it gets resolved rather than averaged away.

The scoring is not the decision. It is a structured way of finding out where people actually disagree, so the conversation can happen there instead of in a status meeting three weeks later.

02

Integrations fail at the seams, and so do requirements

Most of my enterprise work has been at the joins between systems: REST and SOAP integrations, synchronisation between platforms, defect resolution across services that each work correctly on their own.

The pattern is consistent. Individual systems are rarely broken. What breaks is the assumption each one makes about the other: field formats nobody documented, a status that means two different things, an ordering guarantee that was never actually guaranteed.

Requirements fail the same way. The user story is fine, the acceptance criteria are fine, and the thing that breaks is the unstated assumption between them. Root-cause analysis on integrations taught me to write acceptance criteria for the seam, not the component.

03

Agents are a workflow problem wearing an AI costume

Building four AI agents into a single enterprise workflow taught me that the model is rarely the constraint. Each agent was individually reasonable. The difficulty was the handoffs: what one agent passes forward, what it is allowed to assume, and what happens when it is confidently wrong in a way the next step cannot detect.

Which makes it a product decomposition problem. Narrow the responsibility of each step until its output can be checked, put structured outputs and evaluation criteria between the steps, and accept that a workflow of four agents has four places to fail rather than one.

The 80% reduction in manual effort came from that decomposition, not from a better prompt.
